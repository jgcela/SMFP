Mary’s Fiat: Virtue and the art of depending
Last week, I was speaking with one of our teachers about the role of virtues in education. We were wondering whether one of the main goals of forming our students in virtue is to help them become more independent as people. We both agreed that, in a certain sense, the virtues help us learn to stand on our own feet, so that as we grow, we do not have to depend on our parents forever. Instead, we become capable of taking responsibility and caring for others, perhaps even our own children, who will one day depend on us.

At the same time, however, we agreed that there is a danger: that both we and our students might confuse healthy independence with absolute autonomy, as though becoming mature meant no longer needing anyone else. This presents a problem for at least two reasons.

First, the human person is a social being. We need others in order to grow and reach our full potential. But living with others is not simply a means to our personal development. We have been created for communion: to love and to be loved, to receive from others and to give ourselves to them. Our maturity, therefore, does not consist in reaching a point where we no longer need anyone, but in learning to love freely and to take responsibility for the care of others.

Second, God Himself asks us to remain before Him as children: “Truly I tell you, unless you turn and become like children, you will not enter the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 18:3). God does not ask us to stop maturing, growing, and becoming independent. All of this is part of the natural development of the human person. What He asks of us is that, even after we have grown and learned to stand on our own feet, we never come to think that we no longer need Him, or that we can live as though everything ultimately depended on us.

This is, in part, what it means to “become like children.” A child lives with the confidence of knowing that his or her parents will take care of what is needed. That trust gives children a freedom and a capacity to live fully in the present that we adults often lose. The Christian life invites us to recover that trust, not in our own self-sufficiency, but in God, our provident Father.

Mary is the perfect example of this attitude. At the angel’s announcement, she did not respond, “I will do for myself whatever God asks of me.” Instead, she responded: “Let it be done to me according to your word.” Mary allowed herself to be acted upon by God. She accepted her dependence on His will and placed her life entirely in His hands. And precisely because she allowed God to work in her, He was able to accomplish great things through her, as she herself proclaims in the Magnificat: “The Almighty has done great things for me.”

Next week we will celebrate our Marian Festival. For us, it is also a reminder of our call to imitate the patroness of our school in her response to God: Fiat.

May Mary teach us to grow in true maturity: to become capable of taking responsibility, caring for others, and giving ourselves generously, while never losing the trust of children who know that everything they have is received from a Father who loves them and sustains them.

In Mary, Christ; through Christ, to the Father!

Fr. James de Cendra, DCJM

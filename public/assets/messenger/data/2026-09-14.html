Does Suffering Have a Meaning?
A few days ago, I found a seven-year-old girl in the school nursery. I asked her why she was there, and she told me that she had a stomachache. There was a bug going around the school in those days. I asked her if she would like to pray and offer her pain to Mary, so that Mary could use it to help other children who were suffering as well. At first, she looked at me without understanding what I was saying, so I repeated the invitation. This time, she nodded, and we prayed together. That prayer, along with a piece of candy that I told her she should wait to eat, made her day a little bit better.

I could not take away her pain. What I could do was help her discover a meaning and a purpose for it: to transform it into an offering, a gift of love to God through Mary.

Our children need to be prepared for what awaits them in the world and in the lives ahead of them. They will encounter joy, love, and happiness, but they will also encounter a good measure of suffering. We do not help them by trying to overprotect them or by giving them the impression that they will somehow be spared from suffering. Suffering is part of the human condition. It reminds us of our vulnerability and, ultimately, that we are mortal beings subject to death. 

There is something very powerful, however, in teaching our children how to give meaning and purpose to their suffering: to see it not simply as something to be endured, but as an opportunity to encounter God and draw closer to Him. When suffering is united to Christ, it can become an offering of love.

Learning how to integrate suffering into our lives is a task proper to the virtue of wisdom, which teaches us to recognize God and His plan even in difficult circumstances. This is precisely the lesson of the Beatitudes: “Blessed are those who mourn,” those who hunger and thirst, and those who are persecuted for Christ’s sake, “for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”

This is also the lesson behind the feast we celebrate today: the Exaltation of the Holy Cross. We do not exalt suffering itself. That is not the main message of the Cross. We exalt love, which is what the Cross truly reveals. Jesus transformed the most cruel and horrible act of hatred into the deepest and most powerful act of love by offering Himself for our redemption.

And so, whenever we or our children suffer, we can help them learn to unite their suffering to Jesus and transform it into an act of love for God. We can teach them not simply to endure their crosses, but to embrace their crosses with Jesus, knowing that He is there with them and that, united to Him, even suffering can become a gift.

Happy Feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross!

In Mary, Christ; through Christ, to the Father!

Fr. James de Cendra, DCJM
Dear Families,

I arrived at St. Mary after a brief visit to my siblings, just in time to partake in the amazing Marian Festival. The celebration took place on the same day we celebrate the Most Holy Name of Mary.

A beautiful prayer comes to mind by a saint who was truly enamored of Our Lady, St. Bernard. He invites us to invoke her name, to think of her, to look at her… after all, she is our patroness. She will teach us to open our hearts to the gift that is her Son, and to be docile to the action of the Holy Spirit, so that we may embark on the great mission of bringing Christ to others and renewing not only our families but the entire world from within.

St. Mary, pray for us.

In Mary, Christ; through Christ, to the Father!

Fr. Juan Espino, DCJM

And the Virgin's Name Was Mary
"All of you, who see yourselves amid the tides of the world, tossed by storms and tempests rather than walking on the land, do not turn your eyes away from this shining star, unless you want to be overwhelmed by the hurricane.

If temptation storms, or you fall upon the rocks of tribulation, look to the star: Call upon Mary!

If you are tossed by the waves of pride or ambition, detraction or envy, look to the star: Call upon Mary.

If anger or avarice or the desires of the flesh dash against the ship of your soul: Turn your eyes to Mary.

If troubled by the enormity of your crimes, ashamed of your guilty conscience, terrified by dread of the judgment, you begin to sink into the gulf of sadness or the abyss of despair: Think of Mary.

In dangers, in anguish, in doubt, think of Mary, call upon Mary.

Let her name be ever on your lips, ever in your heart; and the better to obtain the help of her prayers, imitate the example of her life:

Following her, thou strayest not; invoking her, thou despairest not; thinking of her, thou wanderest not; upheld by her, thou fallest not; shielded by her, thou fearest not; guided by her, thou growest not weary; favored by her, thou reachest the goal.

And thus dost thou experience in thyself how good is that saying: "And the Virgin's name was Mary."

St. Bernard of Clairvaux

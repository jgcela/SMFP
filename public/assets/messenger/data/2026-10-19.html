What Has Happened to Our Children's Imagination?
Whenever I ask a young child what she would like to be when she grows up, I usually get interesting answers: a doctor, a soccer player, an astronaut, a teacher, a soldier, and so on. However, as children grow older, they seem to become less certain about what they would like to be. Before they know it, their interesting, daring, and sometimes crazy dreams have faded into oblivion.

Then college begins to approach, and they have to choose a career. So I ask them again. Nine times out of ten, the answer is the same: “I don't know.” What this really means is: “I can't imagine what I would like to do.” And whenever they do give me a concrete answer, I ask a follow-up question: “Why would you like to do that?” Three answers usually follow.

The first is, “I don't know.” This naturally invites another question: “Then why are you choosing that option?” Only to receive the same answer again.

The second is, “Because I like it.” This is a little difficult to understand when they have never actually done it before.

The third is, “Because it's useful and opens up a lot of other possibilities.” What they don't say, of course, is: “Just in case I don't like my first choice, or in case I still haven't figured out what I really want to do.”

What has happened as children grow up that makes it so difficult for them to answer a question to which they once responded so easily? Have they lost their ability to imagine? Or, perhaps more accurately, has the world in which they live gradually atrophied that ability?

I think this happens in several ways. First, the culture surrounding them can turn them into pleasers: people who learn to accommodate themselves to the roles and expectations required to maintain the status quo of our bourgeois society. The implicit message is something like this: “Do whatever you want, as long as you don't disrupt the way things are.”

Second, we adults build a world around our children in which everything is under control or, as we might prefer to say, safe. We expect them to behave like little adults, as though they were miniature versions of ourselves. We rarely leave them to their own devices because they are too busy following the schedules we have created for them.

Third, they no longer read as much, because screens have invaded the spaces that once belonged to childhood.

Perhaps I am exaggerating a little, but I sometimes wonder whether we are afraid of what our children might imagine precisely because we cannot always control it.

So, what can we do to prevent our children's imagination from dying?

First, let them spend longer periods of time on their own. This means freeing up their schedules so that they have time to play, whether with other children or by themselves. Children need unstructured time, when they can explore, invent, take risks, and discover things without an adult directing every step.

Second, remove devices from their bedrooms and reduce the number of screens in your home as much as possible. Fill that space instead with books, especially books that introduce children to great lives and noble adventures, giving them characters with whom they can identify and possibilities they can imagine for themselves.

Third, read those books with your children. Watch good movies together, too, and talk about them. Ask questions. Explore the characters' decisions, their struggles, their failures, and their achievements. Help your children discover what makes a life truly great.

In this way, we can help our children imagine greatness, not merely in their ambitions, but in the kind of people they might become. We can then accompany them as they learn to discern what is good, what is true, and what is worth pursuing.

But we must never forget one last thing: God has imagined something greater and better for our children than anything we could ever imagine for them ourselves. Our task is not to decide their future for them, nor to make sure that they follow a path we have carefully laid out. It is to help them become capable of recognizing and freely embracing the vocation God has prepared for them.


In Mary, Christ; through Christ, to the Father!
Fr. James de Cendra, DCJM
Dear School families,

The parade of incredible saints continues this month. It is as if the Church is preparing our arrival to the Solemnity of All the Saints (November 1), encouraging us to desire the heart of a saint.

One of the saints we celebrate today is St. Paul of the Cross. As a child, when he experienced a moment of special suffering, his mother would show him a crucifix and would remind him that Jesus offered his sufferings for us and that we, too, ought to offer for Him our own suffering. This is how his love for the Passion of Christ began. St. Paul recounts also how his father used to read to him the lives of the saints and how this encouraged him to be a better son and better friend.

This Thursday, October 22, we will celebrate St. John Paul II, one of the patron saints of our wonderful school. The celebrations will begin with an all-school Mass. Have you noticed the mural that was painted this summer at the entrance of our school? Our great saint is pictured here. He truly is an important figure in the history of the Church and not only of the Church, but of the world. His powerful word resonated in the whole world. It moved many hearts to truly live their vocation as baptized. He invited us to be courageous, to not be afraid to open our hearts, our society, our economy to Christ. Join us for Mass that day if you are able. I invite you to maybe watch a movie with your family or read on one fact of his life to share at home.

Keep an eye out for other great saints this month. In case you missed it, yesterday was Saint Luke!

I pray that you will have a blessed week. Offering any sufferings this week to Christ, meditate on any aspect of his Passion. Strive to live more fully your vocation as a baptized; open every aspect of your life to Christ.

Join me in praying for the fruits of the Annual Gala this Saturday.

God bless you,

In Mary, Christ; through Christ, to the Father!
Fr. Juan Espino, DCJM

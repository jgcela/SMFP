Are we killing our children's dreams?
Last week, we spoke about the saints, and I am convinced that all of them, at some point in their lives, imagined themselves as friends of Christ, imitating His way of life and sharing eternal life with Him in heaven.

This is what we call “visualization,” a way of exercising the imagination that consists of picturing ourselves performing certain actions in the future, under specific circumstances. It is a powerful tool, not only because it helps us prepare for action, but also because it awakens and nourishes the desire to act. St. Ignatius of Loyola, while reading the lives of the saints, imagined himself following their example and thought: “If St. Francis did this, then I should do it too; if St. Dominic did this, then so should I.”

Visualization is a form of imagination that needs to be nourished by images, stories, and role models. Reading is a precious and indispensable means of doing this, as are good movies. But there are also killers of the imagination. Although there are many, I will focus on just two: screens (phones, computers, and televisions) and what I call “completely packed schedules.”

I already spoke about screens two weeks ago, so I will not dwell on them here. As for completely packed schedules, I am referring to those weekly calendars in which every minute of a child's day is filled with a different activity. I suppose the intention is to help children use their time efficiently, but I fear that behind this lies a certain distrust of what children might do with their free time. And so, we make sure to fill it with “productive” activities.

The effect is twofold. First, children do not learn to manage their own free time, and when they finally find themselves alone, they do not know what to do with it. Second, we prevent them from imagining freely. Imagination requires time. And it requires boredom. Children need moments when there is almost nothing around to distract them, no scheduled activity, no one telling them what to do next. But the logic of efficiency leaves little room for this and ultimately kills the imagination.

Childhood should not be governed by this criterion. It is the time par excellence for playing, dreaming, or simply “wasting time.” It is precisely during these years that great desires are born and children begin to imagine a great life, a life worth living that will give purpose to the years ahead. An excessively busy schedule prevents them from doing precisely this: dreaming of their own holiness.

I fear that our highly efficient schedules unintentionally contribute to killing those dreams. And in doing so, we risk sending our children a terrible message: that becoming a saint is something beautiful, but impossible.

In Mary, Christ; through Christ, to the Father.

Fr. James de Cendra, DCJM
Dear School families,

Yesterday, I had the privilege to celebrate the 9:30am Mass. As you know, once a month we celebrate Sapientiae Sunday, where our School participates in a special way in the celebration of the Mass. Yesterday, our first graders proclaimed the first and second readings, presented the gifts for consecration, and, at the end of Mass, received the Vocations Chalice. Until now, it was parish families who took the Chalice home and made a commitment to pray for vocations. This week, our first graders will pray together for this gift.

It is very meaningful to see our school children worship Our Lord together during the Sunday Liturgy. Aside from bringing about greater unity among our children, this moment is a powerful opportunity to promote our wonderful school to other families.
The following Sapientiae Sunday Mass will take place on December 13, and this time it will be the 8th graders who will be serving. We look forward to seeing you every Sunday, and especially that day at 9:30am.

Have you ever visited the Cabrini Shrine, originally a summer camp for orphans? If you haven't yet, get it on your calendar. Go with your family or invite other families to join you. (Recently, I heard that a few men will be riding their bikes to the shrine. Impressive! But personally, I prefer getting there by car. ;) )

Saint Frances Xavier Cabrini, born in Italy, came to the States as a missionary. She founded the Missionary Sisters of the Sacred Heart of Jesus, and opened schools, hospitals, orphanages…

Her desire was to evangelize China, but Pope Leo XIII advised her to go west instead. She is the Patron Saint of Immigrants. Her feast day is this Friday, November 13. Mother Cabrini died in 1917.

It is worth visiting with your family and praying as you go up more than 300 steps to the statue of the Sacred Heart of Jesus that stands at the top.

So many saints left their country to share their faith with us and to offer up their lives that we may have life in Christ.

I pray that in a century, others will be reading about the lives of your own children, about how they have lived out their life for the gospel and that their desire was to bring Christ to every corner of the world.

Have a blessed week. God bless you,

In Mary, Christ; through Christ, to the Father!

Fr. Juan Espino, DCJM
